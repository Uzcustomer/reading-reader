The Hidden Toll of Extreme Heat on Pregnancy

Extreme heat is increasingly being linked with pregnancy complications, especially preterm birth.
Adapted from National Geographic UK — October 2026

As global temperatures rise, scientists are paying closer attention to how extreme heat affects pregnancy. In places such as Palau, expectant mothers now experience many more days when temperatures are high enough to increase the risk of preterm birth.

Researchers have linked heat exposure with several adverse outcomes, including premature delivery, gestational diabetes, birth defects and stillbirth. Possible explanations include dehydration, changes in blood flow to the placenta, inflammatory stress and altered sweating responses during pregnancy.

Researchers in Australia are using controlled climate chambers to study how pregnant and non-pregnant women respond to different temperatures. Other teams are monitoring women in the Gambia, the United States and India to identify biological markers of heat stress.

Practical solutions include cooling kits, shaded spaces, mobile alerts, reflective roofs, access to air-conditioning and heat insurance that allows outdoor workers to stay home on dangerously hot days.

Prenatal Infections and Autism

A large Swedish study found higher rates of autism and intellectual disability after some congenital infections, without proving direct causation.
Adapted from The Week US — 9 October 2026

Researchers at Sweden’s Karolinska Institute examined medical records covering millions of people born between 1987 and 2021. They identified children who had congenital infections passed from mother to fetus during pregnancy.

Examples included rubella, herpes, syphilis and toxoplasmosis. Children who had been exposed in the womb were more likely to later receive diagnoses of autism or intellectual disability.

The association remained even when affected children were compared with their uninfected siblings. The researchers stressed that congenital infections are rare and that the study could not prove a direct causal relationship.

Overturning a Scientific Law

A classic law of friction does not behave in the expected way in interacting magnetic systems.
Adapted from Popular Mechanics — September/October 2026

Researchers at the University of Konstanz explored a limitation in Amontons’s first law of friction, which states that frictional force is proportional to the load applied to two surfaces.

Instead of ordinary surfaces, the team studied two magnetic layers. The layers did not physically touch, yet measurable magnetic friction appeared between them.

The effect was weakest when the layers were very close together or far apart, but stronger at intermediate distances, where competing magnetic alignments forced the system to keep reorganising itself.

This suggests energy can be dissipated through internal magnetic rearrangements without wear, rough surfaces or direct contact, potentially useful in micro- and nano-electromechanical devices.

The Arrow of Time

A theoretical study suggests that collapsing neutron stars may show a local decrease in gravitational entropy.
Adapted from Popular Mechanics — September/October 2026

The “arrow of time” describes why time appears to move in one direction: entropy generally increases.

Researchers examined mathematical models of unstable neutron stars collapsing under extremely strong gravity. They focused on measures of space-time curvature, including Ricci curvature, Kretschmann scalars and the Weyl tensor.

As the model star collapsed, some calculated quantities decreased monotonically. The researchers interpreted this as a local decrease in gravitational entropy.

This does not mean everyday time literally runs backward. Rather, it highlights how gravitational entropy may behave differently from ordinary matter entropy.

Trouble Brews in the Eurozone

France’s rising debt and borrowing costs have revived concern about another euro-area debt crisis.
Adapted from MoneyWeek — 9 October 2026

Investors have become more nervous about France as public debt and government borrowing costs rise. French ten-year bond yields climbed sharply, while the euro and the local stock market came under pressure.

Markets are watching the spread between French and German government bond yields. A widening spread is often interpreted as a sign that investors see greater risk in French debt.

Some analysts compare the situation with the eurozone crisis of the early 2010s. Others argue that Europe is now better prepared because the ECB has stronger crisis-management tools and banks are better capitalised.

The Brave New World of Digital Money

Former US regulator J. Christopher Giancarlo discusses crypto, stablecoins, tokenised assets and blockchain-based finance.
Adapted from MoneyWeek — 9 October 2026

J. Christopher Giancarlo became known as “CryptoDad” after arguing that regulators should build sensible rules for digital assets rather than simply suppress them.

He believes blockchain technology could transform property records, financial markets and national currencies. Putting ownership records on digital ledgers could reduce ambiguity and make transactions faster.

His Digital Dollar project explores how the US dollar might be modernised for blockchain-based finance while preserving privacy, free enterprise and the dollar’s role as a global reserve currency.

Giancarlo expects stablecoins and tokenised assets to become increasingly important, while decentralised exchanges have already encouraged traditional markets to offer longer trading hours.
